    Whole world

    Start here. Pick a place on the map: click a country, search a name, or draw a box. Everything on this side then shows the fire history of that place, from 2001 to today.

    How fire is counted. The world is divided into squares of about 11 km. A square that burns on one day counts as 1. So 500 can mean 500 squares burning for a day, or 50 squares burning for 10 days. "Typical" is the middle value of all the years since 2003.

    Burning calendar

    How to read it. Each row is a year and each square is one month (or week) of that year. Darker means more fire. The bottom row shows a typical year. A dashed outline marks the month still in progress. Two things to keep in mind: before July 2002 only one of the two MODIS satellites was flying, so those months read low; and the last few months use early data that NASA later refines, so treat them as provisional. Click a square to see its days and where the fires were.

    Day by day:

    How to read it. Each bar is one day. The highlighted bars are the period you picked. The dashed line is a typical day in that month. The red dots on the map are the fires the satellites saw in that period.

    Through the year

    How to read it. The grey band is the normal range for each month. A coloured line above the band means that month was unusually active.

    Year by year

    How to read it. One bar per year. Bars above the dashed line had more fire than a typical year. The straight line shows the long-term direction.

    Two sensors, one record

    How to read it. Blue is the older sensor, MODIS. Orange is the newer one, VIIRS, as NASA delivers it: it reports far more fire because it sees smaller fires. Green is VIIRS after our conversion. Where green follows blue, the two sensors tell one story. Why VIIRS sees more

    How it works

    The problem

    Two NASA instruments watch the world's fires. MODIS has done it since 2000 but is near the end of its life. VIIRS took over in 2012. For the same burning, VIIRS reports about 2.6 times as much fire as MODIS. Put the two records end to end and you get a jump in 2012 that never happened on the ground.

    Why VIIRS sees more

    1. Its pixels are much smaller. A typical MODIS fire pixel covers 1.4 km². A typical VIIRS one covers 0.2 km², about 7 times less ground. A small fire is a big part of a small pixel, so it stands out. Inside a large pixel the same fire is diluted by the cool ground around it and goes unnoticed.

    2. So VIIRS catches the small fires. 46% of VIIRS hotspots are weaker than 5 megawatts. For MODIS it is 1%.

    3. MODIS misses most small fires and sees most large ones. Take every place and day where VIIRS saw fire, sort them by how powerful the fire was, and ask how often MODIS saw fire there too:

    Share of VIIRS fire cell-days that MODIS also detected, by fire power (megawatts). All fires worldwide, 2013–2021.

    The result: 74% of the fire VIIRS records was never seen by MODIS at all. That is where the extra comes from. It is real fire, not an error. It just means the two numbers cannot be compared as they are.

    What we do about it

    We count fire in grid squares per day instead of raw detections, which removes the effect of one fire lighting up many small pixels. Then, for every VIIRS fire, we estimate how MODIS would have counted it. The chart above is the heart of that: a weak fire counts for little, a strong one for almost a whole detection. The estimate also looks at what is burning nearby, how long the fire has burned, and where MODIS would have been in its 16-day orbit. Local and seasonal corrections finish the job.

    How well it works

    We tested it on 2019–2021, years the method never saw, when both instruments were flying. In areas of about 550 km, 96% of fire activity lands within ±10% of what MODIS really recorded over three years, and 90% in any single month. For comparison, two identical VIIRS satellites checked against each other reach 94%.

    In this app the record is MODIS before 20 January 2012 and converted VIIRS after. "Typical" means the median of 2003 to the last full year. The chart "Two sensors, one record" shows the raw and converted lines for whatever area you choose.
